# Brainstorming Question Bank

Ask a handful at a time. Follow up on anything that gets a long or energetic
answer — that's where the essay is. Push for specifics: "Walk me through that
exact moment. Where were you? What did you say?"

## Everyday life
- What do you do on a free Saturday when no one's telling you what to do?
- What's something you've done hundreds of times (a commute, a chore, a ritual)? What have you noticed about it?
- What's on your walls, your phone's home screen, your desk? Why?
- What do your friends come to you for?
- What's a job, responsibility, or role at home that's yours?

## Curiosity
- What rabbit hole have you gone down recently? How did you get there?
- What's a question you can't stop thinking about?
- What would you teach a 30-minute class on with no prep?
- What topic makes you lose track of time?

## Change and tension
- When did you change your mind about something important?
- What's something you used to be embarrassed about and aren't anymore (or still are)?
- When did you fail at something you cared about? What did you do next?
- Where do two parts of your life or identity pull in different directions?
- What's a rule (family, school, cultural) you've questioned?

## People and places
- Who has shaped how you think, and what's one specific thing they said or did?
- What place feels most like you? Describe it with all five senses.
- What community do you belong to that most people don't know about?
- What's a tradition in your family that outsiders would find surprising?

## Values
- What makes you angry about the world? What have you actually done about it?
- What do you do even when no one notices?
- What are you proud of that wouldn't show up on a résumé?

## Red flags when picking a topic
These aren't banned, but they're hard to do well — the student needs a genuinely
fresh angle:
- The big game / winning (or losing) the championship
- Mission or service trip where the student "learned how lucky they are"
- A grandparent's death told mainly as the grandparent's story
- A list of accomplishments in paragraph form
- Moving/immigrant story without a specific personal lens
- Trauma written for shock rather than reflection (fine to write about hard things, but the focus should be on the student's response and growth, and they should be comfortable sharing it)

## Good topic signals
- The student lights up talking about it
- There's a specific, visualizable moment
- It reveals a trait, value, or way of thinking not visible elsewhere in the application
- It passes the **swap test**: no other applicant could tell this exact story
