# Example Essays from Admitted Students: Craft Notes

Notes on a reference set of admitted students' essays: ten Harvard personal
statements (The Harvard Crimson Business Board's sponsored "10 Successful
Harvard Essays" feature, 2025), plus Harvard and University of Michigan
supplements published by CollegeAdvisor.com. The essays themselves aren't
reproduced here. These are notes on technique, in our own words.

## How to use these

- **Teach the technique, never the text.** Point students to a move ("open on
  one tiny, strange visual detail and explain it later") and never to sentences
  or story shapes they could copy. If a draft starts echoing one of these
  essays, say so.
- **Admitted doesn't mean flawless.** These students got in with the whole
  application behind them. Several essays below have real weaknesses, and
  those are flagged because they teach as much as the strengths do.
- **Don't push students toward hard stories.** Some of these essays deal with
  loss, abuse, or poverty. They work because of how the writer reflects on the
  experience, not because the experience is dramatic. Students only share what
  they choose to share.

## Harvard personal statements

### 1. Claire: grandfather, poetry, a blurred memory (prompt #1 or #7)
- **Opening:** One tight, strange visual detail from a memory of her grandfather's face, which we only understand later.
- **Structure:** Fragmented memories of a distant grandfather in Beijing, a turning point (a school poetry recitation contest), then a return to the same opening scene, now seen and heard clearly. The ending deliberately reverses the opening.
- **What works:** She's honest about the emotional distance between them. Specific cultural details (his treat from a Beijing fast-food restaurant, her Chinese name meaning "butterfly") ground it. The grandfather never takes over the essay; it's about *her* relationship to language.
- **Watch out:** The final paragraph turns ornate. That suits a poet, but for most students it would read as overwritten.

### 2. Alexander: learning to cook pho with his grandmother (prompt #5 / #7)
- **Device:** An extended metaphor. Each piece of cooking advice from his grandmother (the broth as foundation, balancing ingredients) organizes a section.
- **What works:** A warm, concrete setting, and the grandmother's advice gives each section a clear organizing idea.
- **Watch out:** The metaphor turns into a frame for listing activities (student government, baseball, a math honor society, lab work, a service trip). The kitchen scenes are vivid; the résumé parts aren't. A lesson in **not letting a metaphor become a delivery truck for the activities list.**

### 3. Barry: a large family, losing both parents (prompt #2)
- **Opening:** A sibling's shouted argument over breakfast. It drops the reader straight into the noisy household.
- **What works:** Exact details carry the emotion without telling us how to feel: his mother dividing crop money into piles for school fees and debts, his father's calloused hands. Dates and specifics make it credible.
- **Watch out:** The final line sums up his identity in a slogan instead of reflecting. The reflection is thin for such a big story, and the essay relies on the events themselves. Students with hard stories should ask "what do I think about this now?", not just "what happened?"

### 4. Dunni: bright yellow Crocs (prompt #5 / #7)
- **Device:** A playful, specific object (a pair of yellow Crocs she spent two months deciding to buy) as the thread running through serious material: a first protest march, then co-founding a student activism organization.
- **What works:** Humor and self-awareness about her own indecision. The shoes come back at every stage. A peer's question about why she wears such noticeable shoes sets up the closing reflection on visible leadership.
- **Lesson:** A light object can carry real substance. The ending mirrors the opening: she becomes, for someone else, the stranger in yellow shoes who first inspired her.

### 5. Isabelle: plain bagel vs. egg bagel (prompt #5)
- **Device:** A small personal habit (always waiting for plain bagels) as a key to her personality: someone who knows exactly what she wants.
- **What works:** A specific, funny voice, including asides about other bagel flavors. She names a real tension in herself (meticulous vs. spontaneous) and keeps both instead of claiming one replaced the other. Becoming a dance director shows both traits in action.
- **Watch out:** It uses the "comfort zone" cliché, and the ending states the point in abstract words ("multidimensional," "versatility"). The dance-director section is where the essay earns its claim.

### 6. Olivia: trees, a plant lab, and a grandfather (prompt #1 / #7)
- **Opening:** A German word her grandfather taught her, meaning the feeling of being alone in a forest. It sets up the theme of being alone vs. connected.
- **Structure:** Two threads braided together: a science path (a plant lab she didn't want at first, gene-editing research, a science fair, starting a mentoring program) and a family secret about her grandfather. They're joined by research showing that trees share resources through fungal networks.
- **What works:** The mentoring details show real attention, like recognizing that a kid asking about snacks may not have eaten that day. The family revelation is delivered as a run of short, flat sentences, which is far stronger than describing feelings.
- **Lesson:** Achievements can appear in an essay when they're part of a larger idea, not the point of it.

### 7. Jinna: audiobooks about the Supreme Court (prompt #3 / #6)
- **Opening:** A witty line about being able to order anything online, including a crisis of faith.
- **Structure:** An idealized belief in the courts, then evidence that breaks it (specific cases and justices' behavior from the books), then despair, then a reframe: realizing how little she knew was freeing.
- **What works:** It shows intellectual engagement through specifics instead of announcing "I love learning." One-line paragraphs act as turning points.
- **Watch out:** Leaning on a famous philosopher's line and abstract talk of chasing infinity borrows authority. The specific case details do more work.

### 8. Carrie: mental walls and games of Horse with a mentor (prompt #2)
- **Device:** An extended metaphor (an architect of mental walls and a maze) that carries extremely difficult content: abuse and abandonment by her parents.
- **What works:** It refuses a tidy ending; she says some of her walls are still standing and that's okay. Repeated basketball games of Horse with a hospital worker who became a mentor ground an abstract healing process in something physical. The focus stays on her recovery and the mentor relationship, not on the trauma itself.
- **Watch out:** The metaphor shifts between walls, a maze, water and swimming, and a basketball court. Mixed metaphors can blur the image; usually pick one.
- **Sensitivity:** This shows that hard experiences *can* be written about when the student chooses to share them and the essay focuses on their response. It is never a template to push on someone.

### 9. Janna: a city photograph and a room full of plants (prompt #1 / #7)
- **Opening:** A disorienting black-and-white image, explained a few lines later as a photo of New York on her bedroom wall.
- **Structure:** Her bedroom as a portrait of herself: city energy on one wall, plants (which started with a science experiment) on the other three.
- **What works:** Self-correcting humor (she admits her dramatic room makeover didn't actually throw anything out) builds trust. She openly says she still doesn't know what she wants to be and makes that a strength. Concrete specifics: the exact experiment, the number of plant species, named plants.
- **Lesson:** An essay can be about an ordinary place and still reveal how a person thinks. A very short final line works because the essay earned it.

### 10. Emma: bridges of paper, language, and tutoring (prompt #5 / #7)
- **Opening:** A short, concrete line about the first bridge she built, a paper-and-glue physics project that set a school record. It sets up the metaphor.
- **What works:** The ending comes back to that paper bridge. Learning Bulgarian to talk with her grandmother is shown through action: studying the alphabet under the covers, writing her first letter without a translator.
- **Watch out:** Precision for its own sake (a distance given to three decimal places), some overwrought similes, and a generic closing about a more connected world. The metaphor gets stretched to cover a tutoring startup.

## Harvard supplements

### Extracurricular (50–150 words)
- **Tennis:** Opens with a present-tense, physical moment from a rally. Good energy, but the reflection names generic traits (endurance, tenacity). Short responses still need one specific insight.
- **Teen Court:** Plain and factual, which is right for this length: what it is, who it serves, the student's roles, hours, impact. **Lesson:** at 150 words, clarity beats style.

### Note to future roommate (no limit)
- Lots of exclamation points, a running joke about the "rule of three," and a postscript habit. The voice is the whole point, and it's unmistakably a real person.
- It turns sincere (growing up in Vietnam under censorship, which is why she's a good listener), then pops back to humor.
- **Lesson:** Informal prompts reward personality. Here the playfulness hides real substance, which is ideal.

### Unusual circumstances (born extremely premature)
- Clear, chronological, and specific: survival odds, learning to grip an oversized crayon, earning colored-yarn "belts" in a recorder competition.
- **Watch out:** The ending tells the committee what to conclude about the writer's grit and potential. The recorder story already shows grit; let the reader draw the conclusion.

### Intellectual experience (writing a musical)
- Opens in the act of composing. Specific about researching Amelia Earhart and how her story mirrors the musical's protagonist.
- **Watch out:** It describes the dream more than the intellectual process. More on *how* they solve composition or story problems would strengthen it.

### Books read in the last twelve months
- A short framing paragraph about how a ninth-grade novel opened up classic literature, then a long, varied list including books read in French, with markers for what was assigned vs. self-chosen.
- **Lesson:** Be honest; mark what was assigned. Breadth and real self-chosen reading speak for themselves.

## University of Michigan "Why this College/School?"

Read these two together. They show the difference between generic and specific.

### Author 1: Ecology & Evolutionary Biology (LSA)
- Has specifics (named courses, the biological field station, a forest research study, clubs) but **surrounds them with generic filler** about an excellent program, making a mark, and a world-class education.
- A study-abroad pick at an economics school doesn't fit a biology plan, which suggests copy-paste research.
- **Lesson:** Cut the first paragraph entirely and start with the AP Bio lab. Every school claim should connect to something the student has actually done.

### Author 2: Ross School of Business (actuarial goal)
- Strong move: a specific course, a specific professor's research, and a link to the student's background (a Cuban immigrant family, healthcare's impact on Hispanic communities).
- **Watch out:** The professor's name is spelled two different ways. Errors like this undercut a "Why us?" essay. **Proofread every proper noun.** Business buzzwords about leadership and a changing world economy dilute it.

## Patterns across the strongest essays

1. **Concrete opening image**: a face in a memory, a breakfast argument, yellow shoes, a black-and-white photo, a paper bridge.
2. **A thread that comes back**: the opening image returns at the end, changed.
3. **Small object, big idea**: bagels, Crocs, a bowl of soup, a bedroom wall.
4. **Honesty about uncertainty or failure**: not knowing what they want to be, admitting some walls are still up.
5. **Voice**: humor and self-correction make the writer feel real.
6. **Specifics over adjectives**: exact counts, brand names, and places instead of "many plants" or "bright shoes."

## Common weaknesses, even in admitted essays

1. Endings that summarize or announce traits (grit, versatility, empathy) instead of trusting the reader.
2. A metaphor stretched to carry a résumé.
3. Ornate or overwritten lines where plain words would hit harder.
4. Generic "Why us?" phrasing and misspelled proper nouns.
