# Essay Structures

Pick the one that fits the material; don't force material into a structure.

## Narrative arc (single story)
Best for: one defining experience with a clear change.
1. **Hook in scene** — open mid-moment with a concrete detail (not a quote, definition, or "Ever since I was young").
2. **Context** — just enough backstory to understand the stakes. Keep it short.
3. **Tension** — the problem, conflict, or question.
4. **Turn** — what the student did, decided, or realized.
5. **Reflection** — what it means now, how it shows up in their life since.
6. **Ending** — echo the opening image, or look forward, without summarizing.

## Montage (several moments, one theme)
Best for: students without one "big" story; a trait that shows up across their life.
- Choose a unifying thread (an object, a skill, a question, a word).
- 3–5 short vignettes, each showing a different facet of the thread.
- Reflection between or after vignettes connecting them.
- Ending reveals why the thread matters or how it's evolving.
Risk: becoming a list. Each vignette must add something new.

## Object / metaphor thread
Best for: a concrete thing that stands for something bigger (a recipe notebook, a broken bike, a playlist).
- Introduce the object vividly.
- Use it to move through different times or ideas.
- Don't over-explain the metaphor — trust the reader.

## Problem → exploration
Best for: intellectual curiosity essays (Common App #6, "favorite subject" supplements).
- Open with the question or problem that hooked them.
- Show how they pursued it (books, experiments, people, failures).
- Show their way of thinking, not just enthusiasm.
- End with where the question has taken them / what they still want to know.

## Word budget (650-word personal statement)
- Opening/hook: 60–100
- Context + tension: 150–200
- Turn/action: 150–200
- Reflection + ending: 150–200
Adjust proportionally for shorter limits; in a 250-word supplement, cut context to a sentence or two.
