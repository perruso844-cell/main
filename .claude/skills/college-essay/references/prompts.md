# Prompts and Essay Types

Prompts change occasionally. Always confirm the current wording and limit
with the student (from the application portal or school website).

## Common App personal statement (250–650 words)
Choose one:
1. Some students have a background, identity, interest, or talent that is so meaningful they believe their application would be incomplete without it. If this sounds like you, then please share your story.
2. The lessons we take from obstacles we encounter can be fundamental to later success. Recount a time when you faced a challenge, setback, or failure. How did it affect you, and what did you learn from the experience?
3. Reflect on a time when you questioned or challenged a belief or idea. What prompted your thinking? What was the outcome?
4. Reflect on something that someone has done for you that has made you happy or thankful in a surprising way. How has this gratitude affected or motivated you?
5. Discuss an accomplishment, event, or realization that sparked a period of personal growth and a new understanding of yourself or others.
6. Describe a topic, idea, or concept you find so engaging that it makes you lose all track of time. Why does it captivate you? What or who do you turn to when you want to learn more?
7. Share an essay on any topic of your choice. It can be one you've already written, one that responds to a different prompt, or one of your own design.

Tips:
- Find the story first, then pick the prompt — most good essays fit several. #7 covers anything.
- For #2, keep the obstacle to roughly a third of the essay; the response and growth are the point.
- The goal is to reveal character, values, and thinking — not to list achievements.

## Common App "Additional information"
Not a second essay. Use for context: illness, family circumstances, school
disruptions, unusual schedules. Factual, brief, no pity-seeking.

## UC Personal Insight Questions (choose 4 of 8, 350 words max each)
Topics: leadership; creativity; greatest talent or skill; educational
opportunity or barrier; significant challenge; academic subject that inspires
you; making your school or community better; what makes you stand out
(beyond the other answers).

Tips:
- UC readers prefer direct, plain answers over literary style. Answer the question in the first few sentences.
- Concrete examples with outcomes. "Here's what I did, how, and what changed."
- Choose 4 that together show different sides of the student; avoid repeating the activities list.

## "Why us?" supplement
- Research is the whole game. Specific courses, professors, programs, traditions, clubs, research opportunities — and *why each matters to this student specifically*.
- Swap test: if you could replace the school name with another and it still works, it's too generic.
- Avoid: rankings, prestige, location/weather, campus beauty, "diverse community" without specifics.
- Connect the school's offerings to the student's past (what they've done) and future (what they'd do there).

## "Why this major?"
- Show the origin of the interest with a concrete moment, then show how they've pursued it.
- Mention specific things they want to study/do. Not "I've always been passionate about…"

## Community essay
- Define "community" broadly: family, online groups, a job, a team, a neighborhood, a culture.
- Show the student's role in it and what they contribute or learned — not just a description of the group.

## Activity / extracurricular essay (often 150–250 words)
- Pick one activity and go deep on one moment, not the résumé version.
- Show what they do, why it matters to them, and what it reveals.

## Short answers / lists (e.g., "favorite book, film, word")
- Be specific and genuine; a little personality and humor are welcome.
- Don't pick things to sound impressive. Readers can tell.

## Diversity / identity / perspective
- Focus on the student's own experience and perspective, and how it shapes what they'd bring to campus.
- They decide what to share; never push a student to disclose more than they want.
